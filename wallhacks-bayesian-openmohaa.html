You Cannot Hide a Player and Show Them at the Same Time

How OpenMoHAA hides players from wallhacks, and why the timing can never be perfect

Essay · Netcode / Bayesian modelling · ~125 min read · September 2026 · by mtc

The simplest defence against wallhacks is for the server to not send an enemy's position until you could actually see them. The catch is that your game needs that position a bit before the enemy comes around the corner, or they show up late on your screen. In this post I go through the OpenMoHAA source to see how early "a bit before" has to be, why it changes from fight to fight, and how I'd pick a setting when I can't measure the numbers that would decide it. --- This post is still a work in progress.

1. Why I wrote this

I wanted to understand how Medal of Honor: Allied Assault (MOHAA) netcode behaves when the server hides players that a client shouldn't see yet, and writing it down is the best way I know to check that I really understand something. So this is my current mental model, nothing more. I picked MOHAA because it's the game and the codebase I know best, not because it's special. Its open-source re-implementation, OpenMoHAA, inherits the Quake III / ioquake3 networking code, so most of what follows applies, with different names and numbers, to any shooter where the server decides where everyone is. This isn't official OpenMoHAA documentation and it doesn't speak for the project's developers. Whenever I quote code, I quote one fixed revision and link to it, so you can check my reading.

How sure am I about all this? I've read the code and traced the paths by hand, and I did the math myself, but I haven't measured any of it on a live server yet. I am fully aware that my interpretation may be incomplete or wrong in places, and some of the things I flag look like bugs on paper but may turn out to be intentional once you know the history. So, keep that in mind. In any case, corrections and suggestions are all welcome. Just reach out to me on Discord.

None of this is a new problem, and most of the pieces are already out there. Yan and Randell's taxonomy described cheating in online games as a problem of misplaced trust back in 2005 [1] (though cheating is about as old as games). A wallhack works because the game hands the client information it shouldn't use, and nothing stops a modified client from using it. Others have measured how much hidden information ends up on a player's machine [2, 3]. The fix this post is about, filtering on the server, just doesn't send that information until it's needed. Several games have some version of it. MOHAA: Breakthrough got one in its 2.30 patch, and OpenMoHAA has its own [4]. Counter-Strike has occlusion culling [5], plus a community project called CornerCulling, and VALORANT has Riot's Fog of War [6]. How early "needed" is depends on snapshot interpolation and client prediction, which Bernier [7], the Valve wiki [8], Gambetta [9] and van Waveren [10] explain really well. Other people go after the same trust problem in other ways, by spotting cheaters statistically [11], by hiding the game state with cryptography [12, 13], or by running part of the client in trusted hardware [3]. And for the statistics, there are good textbooks on Bayesian modelling with messy measurements [14, 15].

What I couldn't find was one place that follows a hidden player along a single timeline, from when the server sends them, to when a cheat can first see them, to when they come into view, to when an honest player finally sees them on screen. None of the individual pieces here is new. What I'm adding is a more mathematical way of looking at netcode, and you can skip that part. It lives in the blue boxes and in section 7.

2. The basic idea Level 1

A server-side anti-wallhack is a simple idea. As long as an enemy is safely behind a wall, the server doesn't tell your game where they are. But it has to tell your game before the enemy comes into view, early enough for the packet to get there and for your game to draw them in time. That's really all there is to it. The rest of the post is about how early "early enough" is, and what OpenMoHAA does about it.

If the server waits longer, a cheat learns less, but your own game also gets less time to get ready.

I'll use one example throughout. You're holding an angle, and an attacker is running towards the corner between you. While they're behind the wall, your game doesn't need to know where they are. If the server sends their position anyway, a wallhack on your machine can draw them through the wall. If it doesn't, the wallhack has nothing to draw. But as soon as the attacker steps around the corner, your game has to draw them right away, and it can't draw something it hasn't received. Packets take time to travel, and your game doesn't draw a packet the moment it arrives either. It waits a little so it can blend it smoothly with the next one. So the server has to send the attacker's position a bit before they become visible, and during that "bit before", a cheat can see them through the wall. If the server sends it too late, the attacker pops out of nowhere, already aiming at you.

What a wallhack sees on two kinds of server

The same moment, drawn by a wallhack on the defender's machine. In both panels the attacker is behind the wall. The only difference is what the server sent.

A wallhack can only draw what the server sent. The classic rule sends every enemy who could in principle be seen from your part of the map (section 3.1 explains that test). A server that holds players back, like OpenMoHAA with sv_netoptimize, leaves the enemy out of your packets until just before they step out. How long "just before" should be is what this post is about.

There's no setting that fixes both problems. Every system that hides players has the same timing trade-off, from an old patch for a World War II shooter to the fog of war in a modern competitive game. You can move it, measure it and tune it, but you can't turn it off. I think that's why arguments about "just turn on the anti-wallhack" usually go nowhere. In the rest of the post I try to put a number on the trade-off, then a whole distribution, and finally say how you could measure it on a real server (that would be very cool).

This post isn't about aimbots, triggerbots or other cheats that only use information your game is supposed to have. Hiding players doesn't help against those. It's also not about the bigger anti-cheat problem of detecting modified clients [16]. It's only about when the server should tell your game about a player you can't see yet. Finally, if you are reading this hoping to learn how to bypass an anti-cheat, I have bad news: anti-cheats do not share the limitations of client-side cheats, since they can rely on server-side information. Good luck with that.

3. Snapshots, interpolation and a corner

3.1 Snapshots and interpolation Level 1

In Quake III and the engines built on it, like MOHAA and OpenMoHAA, only the server really knows where everybody is. It updates the world in fixed steps, one every 50 ms with the default sv_fps 20. After each step it writes a snapshot for every connected player, with that player's own state and the other entities the server thinks they should know about, and sends it in a UDP packet [17, 18, 19]. To save bandwidth, a snapshot only contains what changed since the last one the client confirmed. So when a packet gets lost, nothing is resent, and the next snapshot just carries a bit more [17, 18].

Your machine draws a lot more than 20 frames per second, so it has to draw other players at moments that no snapshot covers. The usual trick, which Bernier wrote about for Half-Life [7] and Valve later documented in detail [8], is interpolation. Your game keeps its idea of "now" a little behind the newest snapshot on purpose, so that for every frame it has one snapshot just before and one just after, and it blends the two. That means other players are always drawn slightly in the past. Your own player is the exception. To keep the controls responsive, your game runs your inputs through the same movement code as the server, shows you the result right away, and corrects it if the server later disagrees [9, 20]. Gambetta's articles have a small live demo of both ideas, and if you want the MOHAA version, I built one here.

Now the wallhack. The server decides which entities go into your snapshot, and the classic rule is pretty coarse. When a map is compiled, it's cut into regions, and the compiler writes down which regions could possibly see which others. That list is called the potentially visible set, or PVS [21]. An enemy in a region that could in principle be seen from yours gets sent to you, even if a crate, a doorway or a thin wall is between you right now. Their position then sits in your machine's memory, and a wallhack is just a program that reads that memory and draws what it finds. The server can't stop your machine from reading its own memory. All it can do is not send the data. That's what the 2.30 patch of MOHAA: Breakthrough did, and what OpenMoHAA does again with sv_netoptimize [4, 22]. The problem is that holding data back clashes with interpolation, because interpolation needs the data before it's needed on screen. If you'd like to see a modern take on the idea first, Riot's Fog of War write-up [6] has two short before-and-after clips.

3.2 A peek, step by step Level 1

Let's take the fight from section 2 and watch the corner from your side, as the defender. Four moments matter, and I'll give each one a letter.

  1. S, the server starts sending. At some server step, the server decides that the attacker now belongs in your snapshot. In OpenMoHAA that decision is a line-of-sight test that looks a little into the future (section 5), while other games may just use a fixed head start. Either way, S is the time of the first snapshot that has the attacker in it.
  2. A, the packet arrives. Some tens of milliseconds later, that snapshot reaches your machine and gets unpacked. From now on the attacker's position is in memory, and a cheat can use it.
  3. R, your game first draws the attacker. Your game doesn't draw a snapshot as soon as it arrives. It waits until its own clock reaches the snapshot's time, so it can blend towards the next one. Then it needs a frame to draw, and your monitor needs a moment to show it. R is the first moment a person could see the attacker.
  4. V, the corner opens. The attacker's body comes into your line of sight. This only depends on how the two of you move, and has nothing to do with software.

The order of these four moments is what matters. If V comes after R, you saw the attacker in time, and a cheat could look through the wall from A until V. If V comes before R, the attacker showed up R − V late, and a cheat had less time, maybe none.

Some numbers. Say the server starts sending the attacker 120 ms before the corner opens, the packet takes 60 ms to reach you, and your game waits another 45 ms before drawing them. A cheat then sees the attacker 60 ms before the corner opens, and your screen shows them 15 ms before it, so nobody is late. Now make your connection 50 ms slower. The cheat still sees them 10 ms early, but your screen now shows the attacker 35 ms late. Figure 1 starts with the first case, and you can move the sliders to try others.

The server only controls \(H\), and only indirectly, since it picks a rule and the rule together with the players' movement produces \(H\). And the same \(H\) pushes the two outcomes in opposite directions, through delays the server doesn't choose and can't measure exactly. That's the core problem, and section 4 looks at it more carefully.

Level 3Box 1What interpolation computes, and why the wait doesn't slow down a cheat

Question: what exactly does my game compute between two snapshots, and when does it have the data it needs?

Number the snapshots \(n = 1, 2, \ldots\), let \(t_n\) be the server time of snapshot \(n\), and let \(u_n \in \mathbb{R}^3\) be the other player's position in it (I use \(u\) for positions in all the blue boxes). To draw a frame at a time \(t\) between two snapshots, \(t_n \le t < t_{n+1}\), the client computes

$$ r(t) = (1-f)\,u_n + f\,u_{n+1}, \qquad f = \frac{t - t_n}{t_{n+1} - t_n} \in [0,1). \tag{1} $$

So \(r(t)\) moves along the straight line from \(u_n\) to \(u_{n+1}\) as the fraction \(f\) goes from 0 to 1 (the code calls \(f\) cg.frameInterpolation, Listing 15). How far off can that straight line be? If the real path \(u(t)\) is smooth (twice differentiable) between the two snapshots, the standard error bound for linear interpolation says

$$ \|u(t) - r(t)\| \;\le\; \frac{(t_{n+1}-t_n)^2}{8}\,\max_{t_n \le s \le t_{n+1}}\|\ddot u(s)\|, \tag{2} $$

where \(\ddot u\) is the acceleration along the real path. Let's plug in some numbers. At the default snapshot rate the gap is \(t_{n+1} - t_n = 50\) ms \(= 0.05\) s, so the factor in front is \(0.05^2/8 \approx 0.00031\) s\(^2\). A hard strafe reversal is roughly 3000 units/s\(^2\) of acceleration, and \(0.00031 \times 3000 \approx 0.94\) units. A player is about 30 units wide, so even the sharpest turn shifts the drawn body by about a thirtieth of its width. For drawing, interpolation is basically exact.

What interpolation costs is time. To draw any \(t > t_n\), the client needs \(u_{n+1}\), so it has to wait until the snapshot with \(u_{n+1}\) has arrived and its own clock has reached \(t_n\). That wait is the render delay \(B\), and only the honest player pays it. A cheat reads the position from memory as soon as the snapshot is unpacked, at \(A\), and doesn't wait for anything. Holding players back on the server moves \(A\), but nothing on the client can make \(B\) negative.

4. Why there is always a trade-off

4.1 Why both can't be zero Level 1

Let's compare the two outcomes. The cheat's window is \(W = \max(H - D_A, 0)\). Every millisecond the server sends too early, beyond what the packet needs to arrive, is a millisecond of seeing through walls. The lateness is \(L = \max(D_A + B - H, 0)\). Every millisecond it sends too late, compared with the full time your game needs to draw the enemy, is a millisecond where a visible enemy is missing from your screen. To get \(W\) to zero you need \(H \le D_A\), and to get \(L\) to zero you need \(H \ge D_A + B\). Both at once would need \(B \le 0\), but \(B\) is a waiting time, so it's never negative, and in practice it's never zero either. That's where the title of the post comes from. As long as your game needs any time at all to draw a new enemy, no \(H\) makes both the cheat's window and your lateness zero.

Figure 1 shows this for a single corner. When \(H\) is between \(D_A\) and \(D_A + B\), both things happen at once. A cheat sees the attacker \(H - D_A\) early, and you still see them \(D_A + B - H\) late. Add the two up and you get \(W + L = B\), whatever \(H\) is. So in that range, the only thing the server decides is how \(B\) gets split between the cheat's window and your lateness. Outside it, one of the two is zero and the other grows by a millisecond for every millisecond you move \(H\). With the numbers from Figure 1 (\(D_A = 60\) ms, \(B = 45\) ms), any \(H\) between 60 and 105 ms just splits the same 45 ms between \(W\) and \(L\).

4.2 Different players, different delays Level 1 Level 3

If everyone had the same delays, there would still be a trade-off, but a boring one. You'd pick an \(H\) somewhere in that range and argue about it once. What makes it interesting is that \(D_A\) and \(B\) differ between players, change over time for the same player, and change from corner to corner on the same map. A player on fibre in the same city as the server might have a \(D_A\) of 15 ms, and a player on mobile data two countries away might have 90 ms, with spikes. The server uses one rule for both. For the nearby player that rule leaks (a cheat gets a long window), and for the faraway player the same rule is late (enemies pop in). Once the delays are random, asking "which \(H\) is right?" stops making sense. A better question is which \(H\) costs the least on average, given what I know about the delays and how much I dislike each kind of failure. That's a decision problem, and it has a neat answer when every millisecond of each failure has a fixed cost.

Level 3Box 2Which lead costs the least on average

Question: if the delays are random, and each millisecond of cheat window costs me \(c_W\) while each millisecond of lateness costs me \(c_L\), which lead \(h\) costs the least on average?

Write \(D_A\) for the random delivery delay of a crossing and \(D_R = D_A + B\) for the random delay until first render. Their distribution functions are \(F_{D_A}\) and \(F_{D_R}\), where \(F_{D_A}(x)\) is the probability that delivery takes at most \(x\) ms. I assume both are continuous and strictly increasing, so each quantile is a single number. If the server always sends with the same lead \(h\), a crossing gives a cheat window \([h - D_A]_+\) and a lateness \([D_R - h]_+\), so the expected cost is

$$ J(h) = c_W\,\mathbb{E}\big[[h - D_A]_+\big] + c_L\,\mathbb{E}\big[[D_R - h]_+\big]. \tag{3} $$

Now nudge the lead up by one millisecond. That adds a millisecond of window on every crossing where the packet already arrived in time (\(D_A < h\)), and removes a millisecond of lateness on every crossing that was going to be late anyway (\(D_R > h\)). So the cost of the nudge is

$$ J'(h) = c_W\,\mathbb{P}(D_A < h) - c_L\,\mathbb{P}(D_R > h) = c_W F_{D_A}(h) - c_L\big(1 - F_{D_R}(h)\big). \tag{4} $$

As \(h\) grows, the first probability goes up and the second goes down, so \(J'(h)\) keeps increasing. It's negative for very small \(h\) (every crossing is late, so sending earlier only helps) and positive for very large \(h\) (every crossing leaks, so sending earlier only hurts). So it crosses zero exactly once, at the best lead \(h^\star\), where the two sides balance, which means

$$ c_W\, F_{D_A}(h^\star) = c_L \big(1 - F_{D_R}(h^\star)\big). \tag{5} $$

If the render delay is small compared with how much the delivery delay varies, so that \(D_A \approx D_R\) and both have the same distribution function \(F_D\), this turns into the simple rule

$$ F_D(h^\star) = p^\star, \qquad p^\star = \frac{c_L}{c_W + c_L}. \tag{6} $$

So \(h^\star\) is just the \(p^\star\)-quantile of the delay. For example, if a millisecond of lateness hurts three times as much as a millisecond of cheat window (\(c_L = 3c_W\)), then \(p^\star = 3/4\), and you send early enough that three crossings out of four arrive in time. If delays were spread evenly between 30 and 90 ms, that would be \(h^\star = 75\) ms.

I'll use two consequences of this later. First, the best lead depends on the whole spread of the delays, so a long tail of slow deliveries moves \(h^\star\) a lot. Second, \(h^\star\) belongs to a group of players. If delays differ from player to player, one rule for everyone gives each of them a different cost, and a rule that adapts to each player (section 5.9, box 5) can beat any single \(h\).

Level 3Box 3When two promises can't both be kept

Question: can I ask for the cheat window to rarely go over \(w\) and for the lateness to rarely go over \(\ell\), and what does "rarely" cost?

Say a server admin writes down two promises. The cheat window may go over some tolerated \(w\) (say 30 ms) on at most a fraction \(\delta\) of crossings, and the lateness may go over some tolerated \(\ell\) on at most a fraction \(\varepsilon\). In symbols,

$$ \mathbb{P}(W > w) \le \delta \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbb{P}(L > \ell) \le \varepsilon. \tag{7} $$

I need one piece of notation, since everything below is about quantiles. \(F_{D_A}^{-1}(q)\) is the delay that a fraction \(q\) of crossings stays below. So \(F_{D_A}^{-1}(0.05)\) is a fast delivery, beaten by only one crossing in twenty, and \(F_{D_R}^{-1}(0.95)\) is a slow first render, exceeded by only one in twenty. As in box 2, I take the distribution functions to be continuous and strictly increasing, so these are single numbers. Now let's take the two promises one at a time, for a fixed lead \(H\).

The window promise puts a ceiling on \(H\). The window is bigger than \(w\) exactly when the server sends more than \(w\) earlier than the packet needs, so \(W = [H - D_A]_+ > w\) happens when \(D_A < H - w\). That has probability \(F_{D_A}(H - w)\), and asking for it to be at most \(\delta\) means \(H - w\) can't go past the fast \(\delta\)-quantile of the delivery delay, which gives the ceiling

$$ H \;\le\; F_{D_A}^{-1}(\delta) + w. \tag{8} $$

So the lead can be at most \(w\) longer than a fast delivery, because the fast deliveries are exactly the crossings where a cheat gets more than \(w\).

The lateness promise puts a floor under \(H\). The lateness is bigger than \(\ell\) exactly when the delay until first render is more than \(\ell\) longer than the lead, so \(L = [D_R - H]_+ > \ell\) happens when \(D_R > H + \ell\). That has probability \(1 - F_{D_R}(H + \ell)\), and asking for it to be at most \(\varepsilon\) means \(H + \ell\) has to reach at least the slow \((1-\varepsilon)\)-quantile of the render delay, which gives the floor

$$ H \;\ge\; F_{D_R}^{-1}(1 - \varepsilon) - \ell. \tag{9} $$

So the lead has to cover even the slow renders, minus at most \(\ell\).

That gives a ceiling and a floor, and a lead that keeps both promises only exists if the floor is below the ceiling, in other words only if

$$ F_{D_R}^{-1}(1-\varepsilon) - \ell \;\le\; F_{D_A}^{-1}(\delta) + w, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad w + \ell \;\ge\; F_{D_R}^{-1}(1-\varepsilon) - F_{D_A}^{-1}(\delta). \tag{10} $$

The right-hand side is a slow quantile of the longer delay \(D_R = D_A + B\) minus a fast quantile of the shorter one, so it's at least the spread of the delivery delay alone, from its \(\delta\)-quantile to its \((1-\varepsilon)\)-quantile. If the render delay were always the same, it would be exactly that spread plus the render delay. With round numbers, say \(\delta = \varepsilon = 0.05\), a delivery delay whose 5th and 95th percentiles are 60 ms apart, and \(B\) around 50 ms, the right-hand side is about 110 ms, while the promises \(w = \ell = 30\) ms only leave 60. No lead can keep both. The promises contradicted each other before any rule was even picked. It's section 4.1 again, just with probabilities, and it's why I think the useful output is a curve of which \((w, \ell)\) pairs are possible, instead of a single setting.

5. What the OpenMoHAA server does Level 2

Everything in this section comes from one version of the OpenMoHAA source, commit 667c50f9 from 2026-04-13, and every listing links to that exact commit (not to a branch that keeps changing). I quote the smallest piece of code that backs up each claim, I never edit what I quote, and under each listing I say what I think it shows and what it doesn't. If the project changes any of this later, the listings will still be right for that commit but maybe not for the game you're running, so check the date.

The word "wallhack" doesn't appear anywhere in the feature's code or commit history. The project's documentation calls it a network optimization, files it under a heading that also says "Antichams", and says it comes from the 2.30 patch of Allied Assault Breakthrough [4, 22]: 

### Optimization / AntichamsA new variable, `sv_netoptimize`, enables a feature that optimizes network bandwidth by not sending players information about others they can't see. I read that as a bandwidth feature that happens to hide information, and the code below fits that reading. It goes out of its way not to make the game feel wrong (a grace timer, a look-ahead, footstep sounds for hidden players), and what a cheat can learn is more of a side effect. I don't mean that as criticism. It's exactly why the timing question from section 4 is the one to ask here.

5.1 Server frames

The server moves forward in whole frames of frameMsec milliseconds, and stamps every snapshot with the time of the last frame it finished, a whole number of milliseconds. There's also a second frame-time variable, sv.frameTime, which holds the same length in seconds, as a decimal number. The visibility code multiplies it by three, so its units matter.

For the timing model, this sets the resolution of \(S\). A hidden player can only start being sent on a frame boundary, so at the default rate \(S\) is always a multiple of 50 ms. It also limits what the server can know about \(V\), since its line-of-sight test only sees the positions from this frame and nothing in between.

5.2 Building one client's snapshot: the viewpoint

SV_BuildClientSnapshot runs for one client every time that client is due a snapshot. It copies the client's own player state, picks the point to look from, and then runs the entity loop of section 5.3 from that point. Two details matter here, the point it looks from and the fact that the client's own entity is included.

There's a catch with that point, though. The server doesn't compute vEyePos itself. It's the player's origin plus an eye offset that the client sends with every command, one signed byte per axis (player.cpp:4513–4515, q_shared.h:1717). The stock client limits the offset to what its own camera does (cl_input.cpp:826–833), but the server reads it without any range check (msg.cpp:1169–1174). So a modified client could move the point the server looks from by up to about 127 units on each axis, for example well above a normal eye, which sits 82 units above the origin. That's how the original game was designed, but it matters for a feature that's supposed to limit what cheaters learn, so I list it as fixture F15.

5.3 Which entities get sent

SV_AddEntitiesVisibleFromPoint goes through every entity and runs a series of tests on it, from cheap to expensive. The start of the loop is almost word for word Quake III. It skips entities that aren't in use or aren't linked into the world, and it respects the SVF_NOCLIENT, SVF_SINGLECLIENT and SVF_NOTSINGLECLIENT flags (sv_snapshot.c:643–669). The ioquake3 SVF_CLIENTMASK check is commented out and marked "Removed in OPM" (sv_snapshot.c:670–680). Then come MOHAA's own additions. An entity attached to a parent is skipped if the parent is never sent, and an entity attached to a player (their weapon, say) is only sent if that player is, otherwise only its sounds get through (sv_snapshot.c:682–689, sv_snapshot.c:716–729). The sky-portal origin gets its own handling, and entities flagged for everyone, level-wide looping sounds and always-drawn entities are sent without further tests (sv_snapshot.c:698–714, sv_snapshot.c:736–740). The end of the loop is where players get treated differently from everything else.

5.5 SV_ClientIsVisible, line by line

This is the function I care about most in this post. It takes the target's slot first and the viewer's slot second, plus the viewer's forward direction, from the viewpoint of section 5.2. I quote all of it, because the order of the checks matters.

As a timing rule, this means a hidden player is sent at the first server frame where either (a) there's a clear straight line between the two players' eyes right now (or, when the viewer faces the target, to a point half the target's height lower), or (b) the target is moving and there's such a clear line between where both players will be 150 ms from now if each keeps going in a straight line at the same speed. Everything else is either the memory of an earlier success (the grace timer) or a distance shortcut (mode 1). Section 5.9 works out what this means for \(H\).

5.6 The line-of-sight trace

Both traces use the same helper. "Visible" here means something very specific and very cheap, just one thin ray, maybe two, tested against the world and against brush entities, with a list of materials that leaves players out.

So two players standing in a doorway, one behind the other, don't hide each other for this test, but a closed door does, both through the area check from section 5.3 and, if the door is a brush entity, through the trace itself. The test also knows nothing about the viewer's screen, so a target behind the viewer passes the eye-to-eye trace as long as the ray is clear. Looking away only changes two things for a target. The second, lowered ray is skipped (dot < 0), and in the far-plane test from section 5.4, things behind you are cut at a quarter of the distance.

5.7 The grace timer

Every success writes svs.time + sv_netoptimize_vistime into a table with one entry per viewer and target (server.h:227), and until that time has passed, the check for that pair says "visible" without tracing anything. With the 200 ms default at 20 frames per second, a successful check means the target is sent in the next three snapshots (150 ms) without looking, and traced again on the fourth. A grace hit doesn't renew the timer, so even while a player stays in view, the server only looks every fourth snapshot. This smooths over a peeker who steps back behind cover and comes out again. If they're visible again at the next check, they're never hidden in between.

For the timing model, the grace timer doesn't change anything about the first reveal, which is what \(H\) measures. It only changes how often a first reveal happens. It does matter at the other end of a fight, though. A player who just went behind cover can keep being sent for up to 150 ms more (anywhere from 0 to 150 ms, depending on when the last check happened), so a wallhack can keep watching them that long after they disappear from your view. I leave the grace period out of the model in section 7, because the right value is mostly a matter of feel.

5.8 Hidden players still make sounds

The documentation promises that "sounds like footsteps will be sent", and that's done with a small queue on each entity and a forwarder in the snapshot builder. The interesting part is where the sound is placed.

So the filter doesn't hide everything. A hidden player who walks sends up to four sounds per server frame, placed at their real position and attached to no entity. If you're listening for footsteps, you hear them from the right direction, which is the whole idea. But a program that reads the sound stream gets the player's exact position every time they make a sound. Box 8 in section 7 tries to put a number on that. I don't think this is a flaw in the code. The feature is meant to stop your game from drawing hidden players, and it does that. It was never meant to hide everything about them.

5.9 What the 150 ms look-ahead actually does

People often describe the projected trace as "the server looks 150 ms ahead, so your client gets the data in time". The code does something narrower. The server checks whether there would be a clear line between the two players 150 ms from now if both kept moving in a straight line, and if that check passes when it builds a snapshot, the player goes into that snapshot. Whether that really sends the player 150 ms "early" depends on the path the players actually take, on where the frames happen to fall, and on whether the target is moving at all. The box below does the arithmetic, and I sum it up after the box.

Level 3Box 4How early the look-ahead sends a player in practice

Question: with a look-ahead of \(k\Delta\) (\(k = 3\) frames of \(\Delta = 50\) ms), what values can the lead \(H = V - S\) take, and when?

I'll assume mode 2 (NETO_ALWAYS), or a target outside the CULL_IN distance of section 5.4, and no grace period still running from an earlier sighting, since otherwise the target is being sent anyway. The server builds this viewer's snapshots at times \(t_n\), one every \(P\) ms (\(P = \Delta = 50\) ms by default). Let \(V\) be the moment the real line of sight opens, and assume that it stays open while the attacker comes around the corner.

At snapshot \(n\), the projected trace tests the line between where both players will be at \(t_n + k\Delta\) if they keep going straight. If they really do move in straight lines at constant speed, that guess is exactly right, so the test passes as soon as \(t_n + k\Delta \ge V\). So the first snapshot that carries the target is

$$ S = \min\{\, t_n : t_n \ge V - k\Delta \,\}, \tag{11} $$

and when the snapshot times are the multiples of \(P\),

$$ H = V - S = k\Delta + \rho, \qquad \rho = (V - k\Delta) - P\left\lceil \frac{V - k\Delta}{P}\right\rceil \in (-P, 0]. \tag{12} $$

Here \(\rho\) is just how far \(V\) falls from the snapshot grid. With \(k = 3\) and \(P = \Delta = 50\) ms, \(H\) is between 100 and 150 ms, and if \(V\) can fall anywhere relative to the grid, \(H\) is spread evenly over that range. So even in the best case the lead isn't 150 ms. It's 150 ms minus wherever the frame happens to fall.

If the players don't move in straight lines, the guess can be wrong both ways. A peeker who strafes out and back, or runs around a curved corner, isn't where the straight line predicts, and the projected test can keep failing until the trace between the current positions succeeds. Then the player is sent at the first snapshot after the line opened, \(H = V - P\lceil V/P \rceil \in (-P, 0]\), which is up to a full snapshot period late. The projection can also succeed too early, through a gap the real path never crosses, which gives an \(H\) larger than \(k\Delta\). So, as long as the projection is either right or late,

$$ -P < H \le k\Delta, \tag{13} $$

and, under the assumptions above, \(H\) can only go above \(k\Delta\) through a projection that's wrong the other way. Without those assumptions, the grace timer and mode 1's distance shortcut can send a target much earlier. One more caveat. \(V\) here is the moment the eye-to-eye ray opens. A shoulder or a gun barrel can come into view a bit earlier, so leads measured against the first visible pixel are a bit smaller than these.

Then there's the speed check. The projection only runs if the target is moving (Listing 5, L495–L498), even though both players are projected. Take the usual corner, with a holder standing still and a peeker running out. In the snapshots built for the holder, the target is the moving peeker, so the projection runs and the holder gets the peeker with \(H\) between 100 and 150 ms. In the snapshots built for the peeker, the target is the holder, who is standing still. The check stops before any projection (the peeker's own speed is never looked at), and the holder is only sent when the present trace passes, with \(H\) between −50 and 0 ms. So the two players in the same fight are handled by different rules, and the one who's moving, who already has the initiative, gets the other one late. That's mode 2. In mode 1 it flips for most fights, because a holder standing still within 2560 units is CULL_IN (section 5.4) and gets sent to the peeker all along, trace or no trace. I don't know whether this asymmetry is intended. It's what the code does, and it's also what you'd naturally write for a bandwidth feature that only worries about moving things appearing. It's fixture F4.

Now some numbers. Take \(D_A = 60\) ms and \(B \approx 60\) ms (section 6.1 explains why \(B\) is about one snapshot period at the default rate), so \(D_R \approx 120\) ms. For the holder, \(H \in (100, 150]\) ms, so the cheat's window is \(W = [H - D_A]_+ \in (40, 90]\) ms and the lateness is \(L = [D_R - H]_+ \in [0, 20)\) ms. For the peeker in mode 2, \(H \in (-50, 0]\) ms, so \(W = 0\) and \(L \in [120, 170)\) ms. If the holder has a slower connection, \(D_A = 120\) ms and \(D_R \approx 180\) ms, their window shrinks to \([0, 30]\) ms and their lateness grows to \([30, 80)\) ms. None of these numbers come from the look-ahead alone. Each one depends on the look-ahead and on the client's delays, which is why the look-ahead has to be chosen against a whole distribution of delays (section 7).

So, the three-frame look-ahead is counted in server time. When the players move in straight lines, it turns into a lead of two to three frames (100 to 150 ms). When it can't be used at all (a target standing still, in mode 2), it turns into a delay of up to one frame. When the path curves, it lands somewhere in between. And it never promises anything about your client, because your delay, \(D_A + B\), isn't part of the rule. Riot's Fog of War write-up [6] does it the other way round and picks the look-ahead to be longer than the expected ping. As far as I can tell they tune one value from average latencies. A look-ahead picked per client, from each viewer's own ping, would be the obvious next step, and the next box looks at what that would take here.

Level 3Box 5A different look-ahead for each player

Question: if the server could pick \(k\) separately for each viewer, what should it be based on, and how would I test it?

The best lead \(h^\star\) from box 2 depends on the delays of the viewer who receives the snapshot. The server does have an estimate of each client's round-trip time. It averages how long snapshots take to be acknowledged (sv_main.c:868–913), in steps of one server frame, and keeps the result in client->ping. (The client works out its own ping separately, from how fast its commands are acknowledged, cl_parse.cpp:380–387.) So the server could use it. The simplest rule would be

$$ H_i^{\text{target}} = \hat F^{-1}_{D_A,i}\big(p^\star\big) + \hat B, \qquad k_i = \left\lceil \frac{H_i^{\text{target}}}{\Delta} \right\rceil. \tag{14} $$

That is, for each viewer \(i\), estimate the \(p^\star\)-quantile of their delivery delay (the \(p^\star\) from box 2, set by how much more you dislike lateness than leaking), add a typical render delay \(\hat B\), and round up to whole frames. It's just box 2's rule applied to each client. The frame residual \(\rho\) from box 4 still applies, so the real lead can land up to one snapshot period below \(k_i\Delta\). A client with a good connection then gets a small lead and stops paying for the look-ahead that a client with a bad connection needs. The best rule of this kind can never do worse than the best single \(k\), because a single \(k\) is just a per-client rule that ignores the client, and it does better whenever the clients' delays are different enough to want different values of \(k\).

There are two catches. A client's ping is a noisy and slightly old estimate of \(D_A\), so the rule should use a smoothed upper quantile instead of the last sample. And a cheater could fake a high ping to get a bigger \(k\), which would be a new cheat in itself, so \(k_i\) needs a cap. Testing it is easy. Two scripted viewers with pings of 20 and 120 ms watch the same scripted peek. They should receive the peeker in snapshots whose times differ by the difference between their rounded look-aheads, and neither should get it earlier than the cap allows.

6. What the client does with a newly sent player Level 2

The server decides \(S\) and the network decides \(D_A\). Everything from \(A\) to \(R\) is up to your client, and the client code is where a common belief, "the client gets the data early, so it can smooth out the reveal", turns out to be only partly true. 6.1 The client's clock

Source-engine games have an explicit interpolation delay (cl_interp, two snapshot periods by default) [8]. Quake III and the engines based on it don't. The client keeps an offset between its own clock and the server's time, and nudges it by a millisecond or two on every snapshot, forward when it had enough data and back when it ran short. The render delay \(B\) is simply wherever that feedback loop ends up.

I want to work out where the loop settles, because that's the value of \(B\) with the default settings. Let \(P\) be the time between snapshots (50 ms), and let \(\lambda\) be how far the displayed time trails the newest snapshot's time when that snapshot arrives. Between two arrivals the displayed time moves forward by \(P\), so just before the next snapshot arrives it only trails by \(\lambda - P\). The client notes that it ran out of data if that trail gets down to 5 ms or less. If it did, the next adjustment is −2 ms, so \(\lambda\) grows by 2. If not, it's +1 ms, so \(\lambda\) shrinks by 1. So \(\lambda\) shrinks by one millisecond per snapshot until it reaches \(P + 5\), gets pushed back up to \(P + 7\), and shrinks again, a three-snapshot cycle between \(P + 5\) and \(P + 7\) in which the flag trips once every three snapshots. (With a real frame rate, the flag is checked in the last frame before each snapshot arrives, so the whole cycle sits about one frame lower.)

So the client draws about one snapshot period behind the newest snapshot it has. That's the normal \(B\), roughly 50 to 55 ms at 20 snapshots per second. It depends a little on your frame rate and gets bigger at lower snapshot rates, and nobody configured it. It just comes out of the loop. cl_timeNudge moves it by up to ±30 ms, and network jitter moves it by whatever the halving rule at L1078 leaves behind. After a reset (a jump of more than 500 ms, for example when you connect), the client starts with no margin at all and works its way back to the steady state at 2 ms per snapshot, which takes a second or so at 20 Hz.

6.2 Which entities get interpolated

For drawing, the client keeps at most two snapshots. The time of cg.snap is at or before the displayed time, and the time of cg.nextSnap is after it. CG_ProcessSnapshots (cg_snapshot.c:410–491) reads new snapshots when it needs them, and moves on when the displayed time passes the next one's time. When a snapshot becomes nextSnap, every entity in it gets marked as allowed or not allowed to be interpolated. This is the rule that decides what a first reveal looks like.

There's one more thing you need to read the timeline right. Entities are drawn from cg.snap, not from cg.nextSnap, since CG_AddPacketEntities goes through cg.snap->entities (cg_ents.c:641–644). An entity that's only in the next snapshot already has its nextState filled in, which any program reading the client's memory can see, but nothing is drawn for it until the client moves on to that snapshot. That move is where the "allowed to interpolate" flag gets used.

6.3 How the client places other players

6.4 A newly visible player, frame by frame

Let's put 6.1 to 6.3 together for a player who was hidden and gets revealed in snapshot \(n\), with server time \(t_n\). All numbers assume the default settings and the steady state from section 6.1.

  1. At \(A\), snapshot \(n\) arrives. Its entity list, including the new player, is unpacked into cl.parseEntities. The displayed time is about \(t_n - 55\) ms, so cg.snap is snapshot \(n-2\) and cg.nextSnap is \(n-1\). The new player isn't in either, so they aren't drawn. But the data is already in memory.
  2. Right after \(A\), snapshot \(n\) becomes nextSnap. The displayed time passes \(t_{n-1}\) (at normal frame rates, in the same frame that unpacked snapshot \(n\)). CG_TransitionSnapshot makes \(n-1\) current, and CG_SetNextSnap installs \(n\). For the new player currentValid is false, so interpolate = qfalse (Listing 11, L291–L293). The entity's nextState now holds the revealed position and velocity. They're still not drawn, because the renderer is going through snapshot \(n-1\).
  3. About one snapshot period after \(A\), snapshot \(n\) becomes current. The displayed time passes \(t_n\). CG_TransitionEntity copies the state and, since the entity may not be interpolated, CG_ResetEntity puts lerpOrigin exactly at the revealed position (Listing 12). In the same call, because snapshot \(n+1\) has just arrived (usually in this very frame), CG_SetNextSnap(n+1) runs right away. The player is now valid, no teleport bit flipped, and the parent and model are the same, so interpolate = qtrue.
  4. At \(R\), that same frame is the first one drawn with the player. CG_CalcEntityLerpPositions takes the third branch (Listing 14, L492–L517). Each of the two positions is pushed along the server-written velocity, from its commandTime to its snapshot's time, and then they're blended with \(f\) close to 0. The player appears at the revealed position, pushed forward along their velocity (usually by the full 100 ms of the cap, about 25 units for a running player, or exactly at the revealed position with cg_smoothClients 0), and moves smoothly from the very first frame. There's no visible jump, because there was nothing on screen to jump from. The whole cost was the wait of about one snapshot period between \(A\) and the first frame, on top of \(D_A\).

That's the normal path. The other path is the one Figure 4 shows when you delay the next snapshot. If \(n+1\) hasn't arrived when the displayed time passes \(t_n\), cg.nextSnap is empty, the interpolation fraction is zero (Listing 15, L853), the flag stays false, and the renderer takes the fourth branch. It guesses ahead from the one known state along the velocity, up to 100 ms after the state's commandTime, and after that the player stands still until data arrives (fixture F8). When \(n+1\) finally arrives, CG_SetNextSnap allows interpolation, and the third branch takes over from wherever the guess had got to, with a jump equal to the guess's error. Box 6 puts a bound on that error.

This matters for the timing model in two ways. First, \(B\) isn't "one frame". On the normal path it's about a full snapshot period, because the client's clock keeps the displayed time about one period behind the newest snapshot (section 6.1), and the revealing snapshot is, by definition, the first one with the player in it. Second, the server's 150 ms look-ahead and the client's wait of about one snapshot period don't know anything about each other. The server picks \(H\) without looking at \(B\), and the client spends \(B\) without knowing \(H\).

Question: when the next snapshot is late, how far from the real position can the fourth branch draw a player, given the 100 ms cap?

Let \(v_0\) be the velocity the server wrote, at the command time \(t_0\), and let the client guess ahead for \(\tau\) seconds, with \(\tau \le \tau_{\max} = 0.1\) s (the cap). The client draws the straight line \(u_0 + v_0\tau\), where \(u_0\) is the position at \(t_0\). The real path is

$$ u(t_0 + \tau) = u_0 + (v_0 + e)\,\tau + \int_0^{\tau}\!\!\int_0^{s} a(t_0 + s')\,ds'\,ds, \tag{15} $$

where \(e\) is any error the velocity already had at \(t_0\) (the velocity in the player state is the one after the last command the server processed, which can be a little older than the frame) and \(a\) is the player's acceleration during the guess. Subtracting the two, the drawn position is off by at most

$$ \|u(t_0+\tau) - (u_0 + v_0\tau)\| \;\le\; \|e\|\,\tau + \tfrac12\,a_{\max}\,\tau^2, \tag{16} $$

where \(a_{\max}\) is the largest acceleration during the guess.

Some numbers, just for illustration, at a running speed of about 250 units/s. A player who stops dead right after \(t_0\) behaves like a velocity error of 250 units/s, so the guess overshoots by up to \(250 \times 0.1 = 25\) units at the cap. A player who keeps running but turns 90° changes velocity by \(250\sqrt2 \approx 350\) units/s, so the guess is off by up to about 35 units. A player who keeps running straight at the same speed isn't off at all. On top of that, an acceleration of a few thousand units/s\(^2\) adds at most 10 to 15 units at \(\tau = 0.1\) s. So when the next snapshot arrives, the jump is at most about one to one and a half body widths, and zero in the common case of a peeker who keeps running straight. Without the cap the error would keep growing. A 300 ms outage would draw a stopped player 75 units through a wall, which is why the cap is there.

6.5 Your own player (prediction)

Your own player works differently. Your game doesn't wait for snapshots to move you. It runs the shared movement code on the inputs it has already sent, shows you the result right away, and fixes things up when the server's version arrives. Bernier described this for Half-Life [7], and Gambetta's articles walk through it with a live demo [9]. OpenMoHAA's version starts from the newest snapshot it has and replays every command the server hasn't confirmed yet.

One thing inherited from Quake III, smoothing out your own prediction errors, is effectively switched off in OpenMoHAA. It only affects your own view, not other players, so I moved it to Appendix B.1, next to fixture F12.

For the timing model, prediction means your own position is ahead of the newest snapshot instead of behind it. So a peeker sees themselves at the corner well before anyone else sees them there. Their commands first have to reach the server (about half their round trip) and wait for the holder's next snapshot (up to 50 ms), and then that snapshot has to reach the holder and be drawn, which takes the holder's \(D_A + B\). That head start is the peeker's advantage, measured in a study by Tokey and colleagues [23] and modelled in a blog post by Yuhao Chen and Tianchi Yang [24]. It's a separate effect from the lead this post is about, and for the holder the two add up.

7. A statistical model of the corner Level 1 Level 3

So far I have names for things and a description of what OpenMoHAA does, but no numbers. The look-ahead is three frames because someone picked three, and the grace timer is 200 ms because someone picked 200. I don't think those choices are wrong. I just think nobody, me included, knows whether they're right, because "right" depends on things that change from corner to corner and from connection to connection, and the only way to know how they're spread out is to measure them. In this section I try to describe what an answer based on measurements would look like. Keep in mind that it's only a model. I have no data from a busy OpenMoHAA server, and every number in the figures below is simulated from assumptions I state as I go.

7.1 Why averages aren't enough

Take the three delays from section 3.3 one at a time. The delivery delay \(D_A\) is half a round trip plus some queueing, it jitters, and a lost snapshot costs a whole period. The render delay \(B\) sits near one snapshot period in the steady state (section 6.1), but it drifts by tens of milliseconds after every clock reset and takes about a second to come back. And the lead \(H\) can be anywhere between \(-P\) and \(k\Delta\), so from −50 to 150 ms by default (and more when the grace timer or mode 1's shortcut sends a target anyway), depending on whether the approach is straight, curved, or against a player standing still in mode 2 (box 4, fixture F4). None of these is a constant, and the interesting cases are in the tails, like the corner where a cheater with a low ping gets a 120 ms head start, or the one where an honest player with a high ping sees the attacker 150 ms late.

And players differ in the worst possible way for a single setting. The cheat's window \(W = [H - D_A]_+\) is largest for the cheater with the best connection. The lateness \(L = [D_A + B - H]_+\) is largest for the honest player with the worst connection. So one \(H\) for everyone is too generous to the low-ping cheater and too stingy to the high-ping player at the same time, which is why box 5 looks at a look-ahead per client. Any evaluation that only reports an average over players hides this.

There's measurement work to build on. Tokey, Chen, Claypool and colleagues ran a controlled study of how the peeker's advantage grows with latency [23]. Yuhao Chen and Tianchi Yang model how a peeker's own uneven connection changes that advantage [24]. Liu, Xu and Claypool's survey maps out the latency compensation techniques whose effects one would be measuring [25]. Riot ran blind playtests to find how many milliseconds of peeker's advantage top players can feel, and broke the advantage into its parts, the round trip plus buffering on the client and on the server [26]. And two of Tariq10x's videos make the same point more casually, arguing that questions about FPS timing should be settled by simulation and measurement instead of folklore [27, 28]. What I want from a model is narrower than any of those. For a setting \(k\) and a population of connections and corners, I want the predicted spread of \(W\) and \(L\), and how sure we are about it.

Bayesian modelling fits this well, because the question is about future corners nobody has seen yet, and the data are noisy, thin in the tails, and measured with imperfect tools. The label matters less to me than the way of working, which comes down to four habits.

  1. Keep the real quantities (the true \(S\), \(A\), \(R\) and \(V\)) apart from how they are measured (timestamps logged on two clocks that are not in sync, and a crossing time worked out afterwards from logged positions).
  2. Pick distributions that match the data. Delays are positive and have long tails, the lead falls on the frame grid, and crossings where the player was never drawn must be kept as censored data instead of thrown away.
  3. Write the priors in physical units, like milliseconds, and check what they imply before looking at any data.
  4. Carry all the uncertainty through to the decision, so the answer is a spread over settings instead of a single number.

The main thing is that a logged timestamp isn't the real timing, and a model that treats it as if it were will be confidently wrong in the tails, which is exactly where corners are won and lost.

7.2 A model of one peek

I find a model easier to trust when it tells a story of how one event could have happened, instead of just fitting a curve to a histogram. So here's the story for one corner crossing \(i\), seen by one viewer. The server sends the target with lead \(H_i\), which section 5.9 showed is the look-ahead \(k\Delta\), scaled by how much of it the geometry allowed (\(\varphi_i\), between 0 and 1), plus a frame residual \(\rho_i\) between \(-P\) and 0. A straight approach gives \(\varphi_i = 1\), and a curved one, or (in mode 2) a target standing still under the speed check, gives \(\varphi_i = 0\). Values in between happen when the projected point clears the corner only a few frames after it could have. The snapshot takes \(D_{A,i}\) to arrive, which is positive, has a long tail, and depends on the connection. Then the client takes \(B_i\), which depends on its clock and on whether the next snapshot is late. Some crossings end without the target ever being drawn, and those have to be kept as censored observations instead of dropped. And everything is measured with some error, because the client's clock is offset from the server's and \(V\) has to be worked out afterwards from logged positions.

Box 7 writes this down as a hierarchical model that shares information across clients, maps and corners, with example priors in milliseconds, and fits it to simulated data. Box 8 looks at the same corner through information theory, to see why a cheat wants time more than bits, and what the sounds from section 5.8 give away.

To fit this to a real server, both sides would have to log a little. For every hidden player the server considers sending to a viewer, the server would log the snapshot in which it first sent them and the test that let them through (section 5.5). The client would log when that snapshot arrived and the first frame that drew the player. The two halves should be joined by snapshot number, never by wall-clock time, and every candidate should be logged, including the ones that weren't sent, because the filter itself is what does the choosing.

Level 3Box 7A model of one corner crossing, tested on fake data

Question: what would a model of a corner crossing look like if it follows sections 3 to 6, what do its priors mean in milliseconds, and does it at least get the right answer back from simulated data?

Number the crossings \(i = 1, \ldots, N\). Each one has a client \(c[i]\), a map \(m[i]\) and a corner \(g[i]\). Let \(\Delta_i\) be the server frame length and \(P_i\) the snapshot period the client asked for (both 50 ms by default). The structure of the model is

$$\begin{aligned} \log D_{A,i} &\sim t_{\nu_A}\!\big(\mu_{A,i},\,\sigma_A\big), & \mu_{A,i} &= \alpha_A + \beta_1 \log\!\tfrac{\mathrm{RTT}_i}{90} + \beta_2 \log\!\tfrac{1+\mathrm{jitter}_i}{7} + \eta^{A}_{c[i]},\\[2pt] \log B_i &\sim t_{\nu_B}\!\big(\mu_{B,i},\,\sigma_B\big), & \mu_{B,i} &= \alpha_B + \beta_3 \log\!\tfrac{P_i}{50} + \eta^{B}_{c[i]},\\[2pt] H_i &= k\,\Delta_i\,\varphi_i + \rho_i, & \rho_i &\sim \mathrm{Uniform}(-P_i,\,0],\\[2pt] \varphi_i &\sim \mathrm{ZOIB}\!\big(\pi^0_i,\ \pi^1_i,\ \pi_i \kappa,\ (1-\pi_i)\kappa\big), & \operatorname{logit}\pi_i &= \boldsymbol z_i^{\top}\boldsymbol\gamma + \eta^{\mathrm{map}}_{m[i]} + \eta^{\mathrm{corner}}_{g[i]}, \end{aligned} \tag{17} $$

with every group offset \(\eta\) drawn as \(\eta \sim \mathcal N(0, \omega^2)\), one scale \(\omega\) per kind of offset. Line by line, the first one says that the log of the delivery delay follows a Student-\(t\) distribution around a level set by the viewer's connection (half the round trip, and the jitter), plus an offset for each client. Both connection terms are measured against a typical connection, with a 90 ms round trip and 6 ms of jitter, so they're zero for that connection and \(\alpha_A\) is just the log of its typical delay. The second line gives the render delay the same shape, with the snapshot period in place of the connection and its own offset per client. The third is box 4's decomposition \(H = k\Delta\varphi + \rho\), with the frame residual \(\rho\) spread evenly over one snapshot period. The fourth says that the fraction \(\varphi\) can be exactly 0, exactly 1, or anything in between, and how likely each is depends on the geometry of the approach and on offsets per map and per corner. The outcomes then follow section 3.3, with \(D_{R,i} = D_{A,i} + B_i\), \(W_i = [H_i - D_{A,i}]_+\) and \(L_i = [D_{R,i} - H_i]_+\).

A few details. ZOIB is a "zero-and-one-inflated" Beta distribution, meaning a Beta distribution with mean \(\pi_i\) and precision \(\kappa\) for values strictly between 0 and 1, plus an extra probability \(\pi^0_i\) of exactly 0 and \(\pi^1_i\) of exactly 1, each with its own logistic regression on \(\boldsymbol z_i\). I need it because straight approaches give \(\varphi_i = 1\) exactly and curved ones \(\varphi_i = 0\) exactly. In mode 2, a target standing still always has \(\varphi_i = 0\) because of the speed check, which goes into the model as a known fact instead of something to estimate (fixture F4). The features \(\boldsymbol z_i\) are what the server can log at the moment of the check, such as the target's speed, the closing speed, what the two traces said, the angle of approach to the corner, the distance, and whether anyone was leaning. In practice I'd also add an offset per session and a slow drift over time, but I leave them out to keep the equations short.

Then there's the measurement part. The client's timestamps are on a clock that's offset from the server's by an amount \(o_{c[i]}\) that the engine estimates but doesn't know exactly, and the crossing time \(V\) is worked out afterwards from logged eye positions, with an error that depends on how fast the geometry was changing, which gives

$$\widetilde A_i = A_i + o_{c[i]} + \xi_{A,i},\qquad \widetilde R_i = R_i + o_{c[i]} + \xi_{R,i},\qquad \widetilde V_i \sim \mathcal N\!\big(V_i,\ s_{V,i}^2\big),\qquad o_{c} \sim \mathcal N(\hat o_c,\ \omega_o^2), \tag{18} $$

where the tilde marks a logged value, \(\xi\) is a small logging error, \(\hat o_c\) comes from the connection's own round-trip estimate and \(\omega_o\) says how much I trust it. A crossing where the target was never drawn within the logging window \(t^{\max}_i\) contributes the probability \(P(R_i > t^{\max}_i)\) instead of a density. Dropping those crossings would make every delay look shorter than it is. For the same reason, every visibility candidate has to be logged, including the ones that weren't sent, because the filter being studied is the thing doing the choosing.

Next, the priors, in units a server admin can argue with. They're examples to check by simulating from them, not recommendations. With all delays in milliseconds, the priors are

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha_A &\sim \mathcal N(\log 60,\,0.6), & \alpha_B &\sim \mathcal N(\log 55,\,0.5), & \beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3 &\sim \mathcal N(0,\,0.5),\\ \sigma_A, \sigma_B &\sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(0.5), & \nu_A - 2,\ \nu_B - 2 &\sim \mathrm{Exponential}(0.1), & \boldsymbol\gamma &\sim \mathcal N(0,\,1),\\ \kappa &\sim \mathrm{Gamma}(2,\,0.1), & \text{each group scale } \omega &\sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(0.5), & \omega_o &\sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(10). \end{aligned} \tag{19} $$

Exponential and Gamma are written with rates, so \(\nu - 2\) has mean 10 and \(\kappa\) has mean 20. The \(\alpha_A\) prior says that before seeing any data, the typical delivery delay is around 60 ms, and it could plausibly be anywhere from about 18 to 200 ms (a 95% range, \(60\,e^{\pm 1.96 \times 0.6}\)). \(\alpha_B\) centres the render delay on the steady state from section 6.1 at 20 Hz. The Student-\(t\) tails let a connection spike now and then without inflating \(\sigma\) for everyone. Sharing information across groups lets a corner seen twenty times help with one seen twice. A prior-predictive check draws whole matches from these priors alone and asks whether they produce ten-second delays, negative leads, or a lateness that never happens. If they do, I fix the model before looking at any data.

Level 3Box 8Why a cheat wants time rather than bits, and what the sounds give away

Question: how much does an early disclosure tell a cheat about the corner, how does that change with the lead \(W\), and how much do the sounds from section 5.8 give away about a hidden player?

Let \(X\) be the target's state that the cheat receives (position and velocity), \(W\) milliseconds before the crossing, and let \(V\) be the crossing time the cheat would like to know. The information the disclosure carries about \(V\) is the mutual information

$$ I(V; X) = \operatorname{Ent}(V) - \operatorname{Ent}(V \mid X), \tag{20} $$

where \(\operatorname{Ent}\) is the (differential) entropy, in bits. That's the number of bits by which the disclosure shrinks the cheat's uncertainty about when the peek will come. Before the disclosure, that uncertainty is whatever the cheat's prior over corners gives, say a spread \(\sigma_V\) of a second or more. After a disclosure \(W\) milliseconds early, what's left is the target's freedom to change their mind during those \(W\) milliseconds, which grows with \(W\). If I treat both spreads as Gaussian, with the remaining spread growing like \(s(W) = s_0 + s_1 W\),

$$ I(W) \approx \tfrac12 \log_2 \frac{\sigma_V^2}{(s_0 + s_1 W)^2}, \tag{21} $$

which goes down as \(W\) goes up. The earlier the server discloses, the fewer bits the disclosure carries about when the peek will happen, because the peeker hasn't decided yet. But the cheat's useful head start is \(W\) itself, the time to pre-aim, to move, or to decide not to peek. So information and usefulness pull in opposite directions as \(W\) grows. That's why the losses in box 2 and section 7.3 count milliseconds of \(W\) instead of bits. For a cheat, knowing early is what counts. It also suggests the server's projection is a sensible design, because it discloses close to the last moment where disclosure is almost certainly needed, which is where \(I\) is largest and \(W\) smallest.

The sound channel is different. Each sound forwarded for a hidden player (Listing 7) carries that player's exact position at the moment of the sound (fixture F10). If the cheat's prior over the player's position is uniform over a region of area \(\mathcal A\), and positions only matter at a resolution of \(d \times d\) units, one sound is worth at most

$$ I_{\text{sound}} \le \log_2 \frac{\mathcal A}{d^2}, \tag{22} $$

which for a 2000-by-2000-unit region at a 16-unit resolution is about 14 bits, arriving at footstep rate. A few footsteps a second are enough to follow the player through the wall, which is what a sound-based overlay would do. An honest client gets the same positions, but you hear them through spatial audio at a much lower resolution, which gives a direction within a few tens of degrees and a rough idea of distance. The gap between those two resolutions is what the channel is worth to a cheat. This is only an upper bound. How much a real server gives away depends on how often hidden players make sounds, which would be easy to count. My reading is that the designers accepted this channel on purpose (the documentation advertises footsteps for hidden players), and I'd measure it before thinking about removing it, since hearing footsteps through walls is part of the game.

7.3 Picking a setting

Once the model describes how the system behaves, there's still the question of what the server should do. Say the model of box 7 has been fitted to real logs, and I have \(M\) posterior draws \(\theta^{(1)}, \ldots, \theta^{(M)}\), each one a full set of the model's parameters. For each draw \(s\) and each candidate look-ahead \(h = k\Delta\), I simulate future corners and record

$$W^{(s)}(h) = \big[H^{(s)}(h) - D_A^{(s)}\big]_+,\qquad L^{(s)}(h) = \big[D_A^{(s)} + B^{(s)} - H^{(s)}(h)\big]_+ . \tag{23} $$

Then I score each setting with a loss that says how much a millisecond of cheat window costs compared with a millisecond of lateness, plus two terms for the tails, because one rare attacker who stays invisible for 250 ms matters more than many harmless 2 ms misses. The loss is

$$\mathcal J(h) = c_W\,\mathbb E[W \mid h] + c_L\,\mathbb E[L \mid h] + \tilde c_W\,P(W > w \mid h) + \tilde c_L\,P(L > \ell \mid h). \tag{24} $$

Here \(c_W\) and \(c_L\) are the per-millisecond costs from box 2, and \(w\), \(\ell\) are tolerances like the ones in box 3, with their own costs \(\tilde c_W\) and \(\tilde c_L\). The setting to pick is the one with the lowest loss, averaged over the posterior draws, and how much the best setting moves from one draw to the next tells you how much the choice still depends on what we don't know. Level 3Box 9How the loss and the posterior combine, and what Figure 6 doesn't show

Question: how do the loss from section 7.3 and the posterior draws combine into one choice of \(k\), and what does the histogram in Figure 6 actually measure?

Write \(\mathcal J(h;\theta)\) for the loss of section 7.3 when the model's parameters are \(\theta\); the parameters fix the distributions of \(D_A\), \(B\) and \(\varphi\). The Bayesian decision minimises the loss averaged over the posterior, which with \(M\) draws is the plain average

$$\bar{\mathcal J}(h) = \int \mathcal J(h;\theta)\, p(\theta \mid \text{data})\, d\theta \;\approx\; \frac1M \sum_{s=1}^{M} \mathcal J\big(h;\theta^{(s)}\big),\qquad h^\star = \arg\min_h \bar{\mathcal J}(h). \tag{25} $$

Without the tail terms, \(\bar{\mathcal J}\) behaves like the loss of box 2. Nudging \(h\) by one millisecond changes it by

$$\bar{\mathcal J}'(h) = c_W\, \mathbb E\big[\varphi\,\mathbf 1\{H > D_A\}\big] - c_L\, \mathbb E\big[\varphi\,\mathbf 1\{H < D_R\}\big], \tag{26} $$

where \(\mathbf 1\{\cdot\}\) is 1 when the event inside holds and 0 otherwise, and the averages are over the posterior and over corners. This is box 2's balance again, with two changes. The lead is now \(H = h\varphi + \rho\) instead of \(h\) itself, and each crossing is weighted by \(\varphi\), because a crossing only feels the setting through the part of the look-ahead its geometry allowed. When every approach is straight (\(\varphi = 1\)) and the frame residual is ignored (\(\rho = 0\)), it's exactly box 2's condition. With the tail terms \(\tilde c_W P(W > w)\) and \(\tilde c_L P(L > \ell)\), the loss might not have a single minimum any more, and the safest method is a plain search over the few whole numbers \(k\) that are possible.

Figure 6 shows something different, the best setting for each draw on its own, \(h^{(s)} = \arg\min_h \mathcal J(h;\theta^{(s)})\). This isn't a posterior distribution of \(h\), because \(h\) is a decision and not a parameter, and in general \(h^\star\) is neither the mean nor the median of the \(h^{(s)}\). The histogram answers a diagnostic question. If we knew \(\theta\) exactly, how much would the best setting move as \(\theta\) ranges over what the data still allow? The two views can disagree. The draws can disagree while their average still has a sharp minimum, and the draws can all agree while the averaged loss is almost flat.

The flat case is the interesting one. Both terms of the derivative above are multiplied by \(\varphi\), so in a population where most approaches are curved or most targets stand still, \(\mathbb E[\varphi]\) is small, both terms are small, and the loss barely changes with \(h\). Then the setting just doesn't matter much, and no amount of data will make the histogram narrow, because the flatness comes from the game itself.

8. Related work Level 2 Level 3

Section 1 mentioned the earlier work this post builds on. Here I go through it in more detail, with what each piece brings to the corner problem, and where I couldn't find anything public. It's a reading list with my opinions, not a proper survey, and it only includes what I could read and check as of September 2026.

How cheating is classified

A wallhack exists because the game gives an untrusted client information it shouldn't use. Yan and Randell's classification of online-game cheating [1] files this under exploiting misplaced trust. Too much is trusted to the client, and only the client software stands between the player and the hidden information. (A wallhack made by changing the graphics driver is a separate case for them, "modifying client infrastructure".) Laurens, Paige, Brooke and Chivers [11] look at it from the server's side and try to detect the behaviour that such information produces. Demenais' history of anti-cheat [29] follows the industry from server-side checks to kernel-level drivers, and one reason he gives is that a server can't see cheats that only change what the player sees, like wallhacks. Riot's 2018 article about League of Legends [16] shows both sides from the inside. League avoids sending other players' state when it doesn't need to, and the article is mostly about hardening the game client itself. This post starts from the same place as the taxonomy. If the cheat comes from misplaced trust, the first question is how much to trust the client, and for how many milliseconds.

How much the client gets to see

Chambers and colleagues [2] studied map hacks in real-time strategy games as a form of information exposure, including the trade-off between exposing less and sending more network traffic. Park, Ahmad and Lee's BlackMirror [3] starts from the same observation as this post, for shooters. A client usually holds much more of the world than it draws, because the server sends players who aren't visible yet so that the game plays smoothly. They also list the weak spots of filtering on the server. It costs extra work for every player, enemies pop in when the filter is too strict, and the server lags behind the client, so it has to be conservative. Instead of holding state back, they move the decision about what may be drawn into a trusted enclave on the client (more on that below). Section 3 of this post measures the exposure in time instead of in amount of state, because that's how both the cheat and the honest player experience it.

Filtering on the server

Not sending what can't be seen is an old idea. The potentially visible set [21] is a rendering optimisation, and Quake-family servers reuse it to decide which entities go into each client's snapshot [10, 18]. For wallhacks, what matters is an extra line-of-sight test per player on top of that. Medal of Honor: Breakthrough 2.30 shipped one, and OpenMoHAA re-implements and documents it [4, 22]. Sections 5.3 to 5.9 are my reading of that code. Counter-Strike: Global Offensive had a built-in server-side player occlusion setting, which I only know from third-party reports [5]. The CornerCulling project, for Unreal, and its Source-engine port for Counter-Strike do the same with a stronger test. They check lines of sight from every position the viewer could have moved to within their latency, instead of only from their last known position (the four corners of a rectangle that holds all possible peeks), and they only hide an enemy if one occluder blocks all four. OpenMoHAA projects each player to a single point instead. Riot's Fog of War for VALORANT [6] is the best-known production system of this kind, and it's open about the two design choices this post keeps coming back to. The look-ahead is deliberately longer than the expected ping, so that honest players don't see enemies pop in, and a lot of work went into making the test cheap enough for the server (from half of the server's frame time down to under 2%). Its final test is a precomputed, much finer PVS with each player's bounding box stretched along their velocity, which replaced ray casts. CornerCulling's test over all possible peeks and Fog of War's ping-sized look-ahead are the two obvious upgrades to OpenMoHAA's single projected point, and box 5 sketches a per-client version of the second.

Snapshot interpolation and prediction

The client side of the corner is well covered by people who build games. Bernier's GDC paper [7] and the Valve wiki's page on Source networking [8], together with the GoldSrc lineage before them [30], set out the standard vocabulary of client prediction, entity interpolation with a fixed delay, and lag compensation on the server. Gambetta's four articles and live demo [9] are the clearest short introduction I know. Van Waveren's account of the DOOM III network architecture [10] is an interesting contrast. It kept per-client snapshots filtered by PVS, but instead of interpolating, the client predicts every entity in its PVS and corrects them when snapshots arrive, which changes what a late first snapshot means for the player being revealed. For the Quake III code that OpenMoHAA inherits, Sanglard's review [17], Fedoryński's protocol notes [18], the Unlagged networking primer [31] and the Swinburne report on Quake III's data structures [19] cover snapshots, delta compression and the interpolation rule between them. As far as I can tell, none of them covers the case section 6 is about, an entity that appears for the first time.

On the peeker's advantage itself, Riot's netcode article [26] breaks it down into the round trip plus buffering on the client and on the server, describes keeping that buffering to about one frame on the client and half a frame on the server, and reports blind playtests where top players could feel differences of 10 to 20 ms. Tokey, Chen, Claypool and colleagues [23] measured the advantage with controlled added latency, and found that it depends more on the defender's latency than on the peeker's. Yuhao Chen and Tianchi Yang [24] model what happens when the peeker's own connection is slower for some packets than for others. Liu, Xu and Claypool's survey [25] maps out the latency compensation techniques whose side effects those studies observe. Tariq10x's videos, cited here by title, argue in an easy-going style that client prediction changed online games [20], that Quake III's netcode is worth teaching [32], that sub-tick timing doesn't solve the problems people think it does [27], and that these questions should be settled by simulation instead of folklore [28]. The accompanying simulator is a local sandbox for the effects section 6 describes. Two MOHAA projects belong here too. One is sh-unlagged, a 2024 wrapper around Spearhead's game module that rebuilds its netcode from the outside, and the other is my own interactive demo of the MOHAA snapshot pipeline, which section 3 leans on.

Catching cheaters statistically

If the server can't hold everything back, it can at least ask how likely a player's behaviour is without the hidden information. Laurens and colleagues [11] do this for wallhacks directly. Their Counter-Strike: Source server plugin traces each player's line of sight, measures how often they aim at enemies hidden behind walls (plus three related measures), and combines these into a weighted cheat score that flags players whose score would be unlikely for honest play. That fits well with this post. Their detector scores a player's behaviour, and the model of section 7 describes the system's timing. A well-chosen disclosure policy makes their detector's job easier, since a cheat with a smaller window has less to act on, and their measures would be natural things to log next to the timing events of section 7.2.

Hiding state with cryptography

The strongest way not to trust the client is to make hidden state hard to read even when it's on the player's machine. Bursztein and colleagues' OpenConflict [33] did this for map hacks in real-time strategy games, with protocols that share the game state between players so that each client only holds what it's allowed to see. Dark Forest [12], a real-time strategy MMO on Ethereum, hides positions behind hashes with zero-knowledge proofs. Players prove that their moves are legal without revealing where they are, and anyone who wants to lift the fog has to pay for it in brute-force hashing. Ingonyama's 2024 post [13] proposes a new cryptographic building block for a turn-based, two-player version of the same idea, and mentions shooters like Counter-Strike as a future target. For a shooter at 20 server frames per second, where visibility depends on a trace between two moving eyes, this is far from practical today, and the core problem of this post wouldn't go away anyway. Whatever is proven, the client eventually has to receive the opponent's position early enough to draw it, and that brings us right back to the corner.

Trusted hardware on the client

BlackMirror [3] is also the reference for the middle road between holding state back and trusting the client. It runs the visibility decision inside an Intel SGX enclave on the client, so the untrusted renderer only ever receives what an honest renderer would draw. On a Quake II prototype, the paper reports an overhead of about 0.57 ms per frame, which it considers negligible. In the terms of section 3, an enclave moves the disclosure moment from the server to a trusted component on the player's own machine, so the lead no longer has to cover the network delay. Releasing the target \(B\) before the corner opens is enough for the honest renderer, and that's also all a cheat gets. So it wins back the milliseconds that a cautious look-ahead on the server costs, at the price of trusting the enclave.

Bayesian modelling

The statistics in section 7 are standard, and there's nothing new there. Gelman and colleagues [14] and McElreath [15] are the references for hierarchical models with measurement error and censoring, for checking a model against simulated data before and after fitting, and for the habit of treating a logged value as a noisy measurement of a hidden one. Vehtari, Gelman and Gabry [34] explain how to check such a model on data it hasn't seen, for example by leaving out one player or one map at a time. PyMC is the tool behind the sketch in box 7. The only thing specific to the corner is the structure. The lead falls on the frame grid and is scaled by the geometry, the two delays are measured on different clocks, and the costs of the decision are uneven in a way the server admin has to spell out.

Between them, these works cover most of the pieces, from what a cheat can see and how game state is sent and drawn, to filtering on the server, statistical detection, and cryptographic or hardware ways of hiding state. What I couldn't find is one account that puts the server's disclosure, the cheat's first access, the real moment of visibility and the honest player's first frame on the same timeline, fits it to logged data, and turns it into a setting. That may just be a gap in my reading, and I'd be glad to be pointed to work that covers it.

9. Wrapping up Level 1

I started this post to write down how I think a MOHAA server hides players and what that costs, and to check it against the code instead of my memory of the code. I didn't end up with a single answer, and I don't think there is one, because the same setting is too generous to the cheater with the best connection and too stingy to the honest player with the worst one. But the structure is the same everywhere. The lead \(H\), the delivery delay \(D_A\) and the render delay \(B\) decide the cheat's window \(W = [H - D_A]_+\) and the honest player's lateness \(L = [D_A + B - H]_+\). Pushing \(H\) up trades lateness for more early information for cheats, pushing it down does the opposite, and whenever both are non-zero they add up to exactly \(B\). The rest is about how those three numbers are spread out on a real server, and that's something to measure rather than argue about.

Given all that, I think OpenMoHAA, in the version I read, makes reasonable choices. It filters per client instead of only by PVS, tests the current line of sight first and only then projects both players three frames ahead, keeps a player it has just seen in the snapshots for a short grace period, and still lets hidden players be heard. I also found a few places where the code does something different from what a comment or a name suggests. I've listed each of them as an observation with a small test attached (Appendix B) instead of calling them bugs, because I'm sure some will turn out to be intentional once someone who knows the history looks at them.

The Bayesian part is only a proposal for now. What it would give a server admin is a statement along the lines of "with these players, maps, corners, clocks and costs, this is what we expect from each setting, and this is how unsure we still are". Figures 5 and 6 show what that looks like with simulated inputs. The real inputs would only need a few log lines on the server and on the client (section 7.2).

So this is where I'd like help. If you know the OpenMoHAA or MOHAA source history better than I do, tell me where my reading is wrong, or which observation in Appendix B is intentional. If you have a counterexample to the timing model, like a corner that behaves differently from Figure 1 for a reason the model can't account for, I want to see it. If you run a busy server and could log even part of what section 7.2 describes, I'd much rather fit a model to your data than keep simulating. And if you think the whole approach is wrong, I'd like to hear that too, ideally with a measurement attached. The easiest way to reach me is on Discord (the link is in section 1), or on GitHub as fecmtc. Again, this is my current mental model of one codebase, picked because it's the one I know best. It isn't the project's documentation, it doesn't speak for the people who wrote the code, and I wrote it hoping to be corrected.

Appendix A. OpenMoHAA vs ioquake3 Level 2

If you read the networking code, the skeleton is clearly Quake III's, function for function and often comment for comment. It has the same server frames, per-client snapshots sent as differences from the last one the client confirmed, a history of 32 packets, reliable commands over UDP, a client clock that drifts, local prediction by replaying commands, and interpolation between two held snapshots. A.2 Interpolation rules compared

This one shows the pattern best. The comment is the same and the shape is the same, but OpenMoHAA adds two more reasons not to interpolate, and a flag. Also notice how nextState is filled. ioquake3 uses memcpy and keeps the plain struct copy in a comment, while OpenMoHAA uses the struct copy.

For a first reveal both engines agree that an entity that wasn't in the previous snapshot isn't interpolated. The MOHAA additions matter for a different kind of jump, when an entity's parent or model changes (a weapon that its owner drops, for example, stays the same entity but loses its parent), and for the teleported flag that lets CG_ResetEntity skip resetting its animation.

A.3 Trajectories compared

Here MOHAA's own state makes the code quite different. Quake III sends a typed trajectory with a base position and an optional duration, and its evaluator switches on the type. In ioquake3, players arrive as TR_LINEAR_STOP when the server's g_smoothClients is on, and a client with its default cg_smoothClients 0 turns them into TR_INTERPOLATE, which means "just use the base position". MOHAA sends a velocity and a command time, and its evaluator is one straight line with a cap.

The code that uses the trajectory is different too. ioquake3's position function has three exits and no cap of its own. A.4 The visibility filter, which ioquake3 doesn't have

The third comparison is the one this whole post is about. ioquake3's entity loop has no per-player geometric test at all. After the flag checks, the double-add guard and the shortcut for broadcast entities, it does the area check, then the PVS, and then adds the entity. Its snapshot builder also leaves out the viewer's own entity, and looks from the player's origin plus the view height. Diff 3 lines up three matching pieces of the two builders.

In ioquake3, that's all a server does to decide whether you learn where another player is. It checks whether the entity is in an area connected to yours, and whether one of its clusters is in your PVS. With that rule, wallhacks are cheap. OpenMoHAA adds a distance and direction test for every entity, a per-player line-of-sight test with memory and a look-ahead, and a sound channel for the players it holds back, and it runs these tests from an eye position the client reports instead of from the origin. None of that exists in ioquake3. The client code that has to deal with the consequences (sections 6.2 to 6.4) keeps Quake III's rule for new entities and its clock, and only the trajectory handling of section 6.3 changed. So the server holds players back for reasons the client was never designed around, and that mismatch is why I think the timing model of sections 3 and 4 is the right way to look at this engine in particular.

A few more pointers if you want to go further. Neil Toronto's Unlagged mod for Quake III, whose networking primer [31] I use as background, is the classic treatment of the other half of the fairness problem, lag compensation for hits, which this post leaves alone. For Spearhead, sh-unlagged is a wrapper around the game library that aims to rebuild and improve its netcode. Van Waveren's DOOM III paper [10] describes id's next architecture, which kept per-client PVS filtering but predicts every entity in the client's PVS instead of interpolating. And the netcodesimulator project is a small sandbox for the same kind of timing reasoning, independent of any engine.

B.1 Prediction errors that are never smoothed (F12)

One feature inherited from Quake III no longer gets any input. ioquake3 measures the difference between where it predicted your player and where the server says you are, stores it in cg.predictedError, and fades it out over cg_errordecay milliseconds, so that corrections look smooth (cg_predict.c:522–571). OpenMoHAA's view code still applies that fade.
